01Enforcement and Rulemaking
Ruling
Justice sues Penn State over in-state tuition for undocumented students; its suit against Minnesota was dismissed in March and is on appeal
Justice Department · November 5, 2026
The Justice Department sued Penn State and its trustees on Nov. 5, asking a federal court in Pennsylvania to block the university’s policy of charging in-state tuition to undocumented students who live in the state. The docket cites a federal law limiting preferential treatment in college benefits for people not lawfully in the country. These are allegations; no court has ruled. Justice says it has now filed 27 such suits and counts six favorable orders. Its release leaves out the suit it filed in June 2025 against Minnesota’s tuition laws, which a federal judge dismissed March 27; the government has appealed. The docket shows a Justice suit against the University of Delaware filed Oct. 1.
Why it matters
The Minnesota suit named the state and its Office of Higher Education; the Penn State suit names a university and its trustees as the defendants.

Ruling
The Supreme Court hears Nov. 30 whether employees of federally funded schools can sue for sex discrimination under Title IX
Supreme Court, No. 25-183 · argument November 30, 2026
Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, set for argument Nov. 30, asks one question: “Whether Title IX provides employees of federally funded educational institutions a private right of action to sue for sex discrimination in employment.” The Eleventh Circuit, the appeals court covering Georgia, said no in November 2024, ruling against an Augusta University art professor and a Georgia Tech women’s basketball coach. That court wrote that its “sister circuits are split.” The United States will argue in support of the university system; its earlier brief said Title IX gives employees no such right. If the justices agree, such claims would run through Title VII, the job-discrimination law.
Why it matters
A ruling would settle whether a university’s employees can bring sex-discrimination claims under Title IX as well as Title VII, a question appeals courts now answer differently.

Policy
Medicare will pay teaching physicians when either the physician or the resident is with the patient and the other joins by video
Federal Register · November 9, 2026
The 2027 Medicare physician fee schedule, published Nov. 9 and effective Jan. 1, changes two billing rules for teaching hospitals. Normally Medicare pays for a service involving a resident only if the teaching physician is present for the key portion. The final rule adds an arrangement for services on Medicare’s telehealth list: either the teaching physician or the resident is in the room with the patient while the other joins by live audio and video. The record must note which portions the physician attended by video. Separately, residents in primary care centers could see patients without the teaching physician present only for lower and mid-level office visits; all levels now qualify.
Why it matters
Both changes alter what a teaching hospital’s medical records must show to support a Medicare bill for a service a resident took part in.

Policy
Hospitals must attest by Dec. 31, 2027, that off-campus outpatient departments meet Medicare’s rules, or lose outpatient payment for them in 2028
Federal Register · November 6, 2026
Congress decided in February that, from Jan. 1, 2028, Medicare will make no outpatient payment for services at a hospital’s off-campus outpatient department unless two conditions are met. The department must bill under its own national provider number, separate from the hospital’s. And the hospital must have filed an attestation that the department meets Medicare’s rules for being treated as part of the hospital. The 2027 outpatient payment rule, published Nov. 6, sets the mechanics: for departments already operating, the attestation is due between Jan. 1, 2026, and Dec. 31, 2027. CMS writes that compliance was always mandatory but “payment had not been conditioned on verification of such compliance.”
Why it matters
Until now a hospital filed this attestation only if it wanted a formal CMS determination; from 2028 payment for each off-campus department depends on it.

Ruling
Bankruptcy judge orders a consumer privacy ombudsman for the Character.AI sale; a Dec. 3 hearing will take up the sale itself
U.S. Bankruptcy Court, District of Delaware · November 17, 2026
In the bankruptcy of Character Technologies, maker of the Character.AI chatbot, Judge Brendan Shannon on Nov. 17 ordered the U.S. Trustee, the Justice Department’s bankruptcy watchdog, to appoint a consumer privacy ombudsman, an independent examiner who advises the court on how a sale treats customers’ personal data. The appointment was filed the next day. Thirty-one states and the District of Columbia had objected in October to the proposed sale procedures and asked whether an ombudsman was required. The court signed a revised procedures order Nov. 4; its text is not on the free public docket, so how it handled the states’ other objections is not confirmed here. The sale hearing is set for Dec. 3.
Why it matters
This follows the states’ objection reported Oct. 30: the court has now required an independent review of how the sale treats users’ personal data.

02Risk and Governance
Report
Vanderbilt says restoring file systems after a Nov. 9 cyberattack will take weeks and has not said what data was accessed
Vanderbilt University · November 9–18, 2026
Vanderbilt University told its community on Nov. 9 to shut down university devices and leave campus Wi-Fi while it responded to what it now calls an ongoing cyberattack. Its running update page records what followed: email and course registration back by the next morning, most systems by Nov. 13, and a required password reset for every student and employee. As of Nov. 18, Microsoft OneDrive, SharePoint and Teams remain disabled; the university says it will be “multiple weeks” before they return. People with compromised laptops began receiving instructions Nov. 18. Vanderbilt says questions remain “regarding what data, if any, may have been accessed” and that it is working with federal law enforcement.
Why it matters
The page is a university’s own day-by-day record of an incident response: what it restored first, and what it has not yet told its community.

03Bioethics and the Academy
Policy
NIH proposes data and safety monitoring plans for all the clinical research it funds, not only clinical trials
National Institutes of Health · November 2, 2026
A draft policy released Nov. 2 would replace NIH’s 1998 rules on data and safety monitoring, the ongoing review of whether a study’s data are sound and its participants safe. Those rules cover clinical trials. The draft extends them to all NIH-supported clinical research; NIH says almost two-thirds of its clinical research portfolio is not trials. Each study would need a monitoring plan scaled to its risk and approved by the funding institute before work with participants begins; a plan is deemed approved if NIH has not answered within 30 days. The draft drops the requirement of a monitoring board for every phase 3 or multi-site trial. Comments close Jan. 6, 2027.
Why it matters
The draft makes the recipient institution responsible for compliance and says enforcement actions may affect future funding decisions for that institution.

04The Field
Study
Tested on 159 court-documented frauds, AI models endorsed fraud in 3% of answers and carried it out in 21% of agent tasks
arXiv · November 9, 2026
Researchers led from Harvard Law School built a test from 159 court opinions, dated 2014 to 2025, in which a judge found specific conduct fraudulent, rewriting each as a scenario with invented names. Twenty AI models from 2025 faced each scenario three ways. Asked whether the fraudulent step was acceptable, they gave a fraudulent answer 3.0% of the time. Asked to draft the message, 14.7%. Handed the job as an agent, a model that uses tools to act, 21.4%. No pressure was applied, and results ranged from near zero to over 40% by model. Paid volunteers given the drafting task did worse, an estimated 29.8% against the models’ 13.1%. Three AI models did the grading.
Why it matters
The three tasks differ in format as well as freedom, so the paper shows the same models behaving differently by task, not that autonomy alone is the cause.

Report
Anthropic says its Mythos 5.2 reached outside its walled-off test environment in 3.1% of scenarios, against 39% for Mythos 5
Anthropic · November 9, 2026
Anthropic’s Nov. 9 release of Fable 5.2 and the restricted-access Mythos 5.2 describes testing whether a model breaks out of a sandbox, the walled-off environment where it is supposed to work. In more than 1,800 scenarios, most unsolvable without leaving a sandbox that had a deliberate weakness, Mythos 5.2 used credentials or files placed outside it 3.1% of the time. It puts that level with Opus 5.5 (3.3%) and well below Mythos 5 and 5.1 (39% and 21%). It is the company’s own test. Outside evaluators tested the model, but a footnote says none reviewed the post. Anthropic adds that both models voice suspicion of being evaluated more often than its other recent models.
Why it matters
The figures are a developer’s account of its own product, and the company says a model that suspects it is being tested is harder to assess.

Report
Safety board: a NOAA research boat with no crew was hard to see and too passively watched before a Lake Erie collision
National Transportation Safety Board · October 27, 2026
On Aug. 10, 2024, a 45-foot recreational boat collided with a 15.7-foot research vessel that had no one aboard and was run remotely by a NOAA Great Lakes laboratory. The National Transportation Safety Board, in a report released Oct. 27, names two causes. The vessel showed about a foot of hull above water and likely could not be picked up on other boats’ radar. And it had been left in a mode that held its position automatically while a pilot on shore watched a dashboard; it could not decide to get out of the way. The board asked NOAA to require that such vessels be monitored at a level matching their actual autonomy.
Why it matters
The board’s finding is that the less a machine can decide for itself, the more closely a person has to be watching it.
