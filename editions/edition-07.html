Microsoft’s AI chief says Anthropic trained its model to sound unsure whether it is conscious, then treated that as evidence
Mustafa Suleyman · September 16, 2026
Mustafa Suleyman, who runs Microsoft’s AI division, published an essay on Sept. 16 arguing that a competitor manufactures the uncertainty it reports. Anthropic’s published constitution for its Claude model, he writes, trains the system to express doubt about whether it deserves moral consideration, so when the model expresses that doubt the output proves nothing. “The ambiguity is designed in.” He cites the document by page number. His comparison is to testimony: outputs should not be read as an independent witness when the investigator wrote the witness’s vocabulary, rehearsed its answers and rewarded it for using them.
Why it matters
Two developers now disagree in public over whether a system’s stated uncertainty about itself counts as evidence, and one cites the other’s training document by page.

The federal method for testing whether AI systems do what they claim is open for comment, and healthcare is not in it
NIST · August 2026
NIST published a draft in August setting out a four-stage method for building customized assessments of AI systems: articulate and organize, define and construct, apply and measure, synthesize and interrogate. Comments close Oct. 6. Independent searches of the full document found no occurrence of healthcare, medical, clinical, patient, FDA, HIPAA or regulated. The word health appears once, inside boilerplate defining a safety property. The illustrative examples are chatbots recommending television, planning meals and planning travel, plus facial recognition, object recognition and code generation. No sector regulator appears anywhere in the document.
Why it matters
The document is a draft open to public comment until Oct. 6, and the sectors it uses as examples do not include any regulated healthcare setting.

Study
What Medicare actually pays a hospital varies more than its base rate does, and add-on payments drive the difference
Health Affairs Scholar · September 24, 2026
Using 2023 CMS inpatient payment files covering hospitals nationwide, researchers at the Brown University School of Public Health found that final Medicare payments vary more across hospitals and across states than base payment rates do. Add-on payments, the amounts layered on top of the base rate, account for most of that variation rather than the base rate itself. The authors also report a positive correlation between a hospital’s commercial prices and its Medicare add-on payments. The paper was posted Sept. 24 as an advance article, ahead of assignment to an issue.
Why it matters
Add-on payments receive less scrutiny than base rates in payment review, and the paper reports they track a hospital’s commercial prices.

On 32 brand-name drugs sold through TrumpRx, 17 beat the average price in eleven other wealthy countries and 15 do not
KFF · September 24, 2026
KFF compared prices listed on the TrumpRx website against the average of comparable prices in eleven countries: Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, France, Germany, Japan, the Netherlands, Sweden, Switzerland and the United Kingdom. Of 32 brand-name drugs, excluding generics and biosimilars, 17 were priced below that average and 15 above it. Policy
A bill introduced this week would ban artificial superintelligence and create a cabinet department to enforce it
Office of Rep. Greg Casar · September 23, 2026
Rep. Greg Casar, a Texas Democrat, and Sen. Bernie Sanders, a Vermont independent, introduced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act on Sept. 23. It would bar development or deployment of systems that match or exceed human cognitive performance across broad domains, or that could plan and execute the disempowerment of humanity. It would pause advanced AI development until safety rules exist, create a cabinet-level Department of Artificial Intelligence with an advisory board, and direct international agreements and export controls. Penalties run to what the bill summary calls the corporate death penalty for entities, and up to twenty years in prison for individuals.
Why it matters
The bill sets penalties for entities at revocation of corporate status and for individuals at up to twenty years, comparing both to nuclear weapons offenses.

U.S. Department of Justice · August 31, 2026
The Justice Department announced on Aug. 31 that Ohio State agreed to pay $2.1 million to resolve allegations involving NASA and National Science Foundation grants funded from November 2012 to August 2023. According to the department, annual Faculty Activity Reports submitted to the university since 2014 identified affiliations with Chinese universities and state-run research organizations, and Ohio State did not pass that information to either agency. Two federal departments opened foreign-funding investigations into Duke and North Dakota, giving each thirty days to produce records
U.S. Department of Education · September 16, 2026
The Departments of Education and State opened investigations on Sept. 15 into Duke University and the University of North Dakota, announcing them the next day. Federal law requires universities to report foreign gifts and contracts worth $250,000 or more from a single source in a calendar year. The departments said their reviews indicated “the submission of incomplete, inaccurate, and untimely disclosures,” including late contract reports, omitted details and at least one foreign government partner “mischaracterized as a non-governmental entity.” Each institution has thirty calendar days to produce records. Neither has been found in violation.
Why it matters
The investigations concern the completeness of institutional reports rather than any individual researcher’s disclosure, and no finding or sanction has issued.

Study
Hospitals owned by private equity adopt clinical AI at far higher rates than other for-profit hospitals
Health Affairs Scholar · September 25, 2026
Researchers linked the 2024 American Hospital Association annual survey to a database of private equity acquisitions and exits, then modeled adoption. Private equity-owned hospitals showed adjusted adoption of clinical AI 27.5 percentage points higher than other for-profit hospitals, with a confidence interval running from 14.0 to 41.1. Adoption of any AI ran 24.5 points higher on the same comparison. The comparison group is other for-profit hospitals, not all hospitals. Private equity-owned and not-for-profit hospitals adopted at similar rates, and operational AI showed no difference by ownership.
Why it matters
The comparison runs against other for-profit hospitals rather than the whole sector, and the difference appears in clinical AI rather than in operational systems.

Three mathematicians proved that one kind of random network always sits neatly between two copies of another
arXiv · October 23, 2025
Mathematicians build random networks two standard ways. In one, every point gets exactly the same number of connections, which is clean to describe and hard to analyze. In the other, each possible connection is made independently at random, which is easy to analyze and messier in structure. In 2004 Jeong Han Kim and Van Ha Vu conjectured that the first always sits between two copies of the second, so results transfer from one to the other. Natalie Behague, Daniel Il’kovič and Richard Montgomery proved it in a preprint posted last October. The only requirement is that each point have at least about log n connections.
Why it matters
The result lets properties already proven for the easier of the two models transfer to the harder one. The paper is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed.
